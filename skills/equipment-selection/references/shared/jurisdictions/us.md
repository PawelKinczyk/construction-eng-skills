# Jurisdiction: United States

Status: draft, reviewed 2026-10. Federal level plus the method to find state and local adoption. Items marked "to verify" were not read from an official source.

Model codes are adopted per state and city. Always check the local adoption and edition. There is no single national building code.

## Legal hierarchy

1. **Federal law and regulations** (Code of Federal Regulations, CFR). They apply nationwide to their subject: workplace safety (OSHA), civil rights and accessibility (ADA, Fair Housing Act), environment (EPA), federal energy rules.
2. **State law.** States regulate building construction, licensing of designers and contractors, and adopt model codes. Some states adopt a statewide code. Some leave adoption to cities and counties. Which model applies: to verify per state.
3. **Local law (city, county).** Local amendments to the state code, zoning, and local permits. Local rules may be stricter than the state code where state law allows. To verify per location.
4. **Model codes and standards** (ICC, NFPA, ASHRAE, ASCE). They are not law until a state or local body adopts them, usually by reference and with a named edition and amendments.

The **Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ)** is the body that enforces the adopted code and approves the design and the work, usually the local building department and the fire marshal. The AHJ interpretation decides. When rules conflict, the stricter applicable requirement normally governs, and federal law (for example ADA) applies in addition to the building code. Confirm with the AHJ (to verify).

Federal OSHA: a state with an OSHA-approved State Plan enforces its own rules, which must be at least as effective as federal OSHA (read on osha.gov/stateplans). Per the OSHA page read, 22 State Plans cover private and public employers. Seven cover only state and local government workers: Connecticut, Illinois, Maine, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New York and the U.S. Virgin Islands. Federal OSHA covers private employers there. Check the current list.

## Official legal sources

**Law only from official government sources.** Use only the domains below to read, check or cite law, regulations and official interpretations. Never use news sites, blogs, law firm articles, training providers or vendor pages as a legal source. When a web search tool supports a domain filter, set it to these domains. If the official source cannot be reached, mark the item "to verify".

How to find the adopted code for a project:
1. Find the state energy code status page on energycodes.gov. It lists the adopted residential and commercial editions (IECC, ASHRAE 90.1), effective dates and whether the code is mandatory statewide. Example read in 2026-10: the Texas page listed 2015 IECC (residential, effective 09/01/2016) and 2015 IECC with ASHRAE 90.1-2013 (commercial, effective 11/01/2016). Pages change. Check the current page (to verify).
2. For the building, mechanical, plumbing, electrical and fire codes, open the state building code agency or legislature `.gov` site, or the city or county building department site. Find the adopted edition and local amendments.
3. Check the date of the permit application. The code edition in force at application usually governs (to verify with the AHJ).
4. Ask the AHJ in writing when the edition or an amendment is unclear.

### Accessibility (ADA and Fair Housing)

- The Access Board writes the guidelines. DOJ (and DOT for transport) issues the enforceable ADA Standards (read on access-board.gov). The 2010 ADA Standards have ten chapters (Application and Administration; Scoping; Building Blocks; Accessible Routes; General Site and Building Elements; Plumbing Elements and Facilities; Communication Elements and Features; Special Rooms, Spaces, and Elements; Built-In Elements; Recreational Facilities).
- The 2010 Standards are mandatory for projects that start on or after 15 Mar 2012 (read on access-board.gov). The 1991 Standards were adopted 26 Jul 1991. When the 1991 Standards still apply, and the safe harbor rules for existing elements: to verify on ada.gov.
- Title II covers state and local governments. Title III covers businesses open to the public. Federal facilities fall under the Architectural Barriers Act (to verify).
- Fair Housing Act: covered multifamily buildings have four or more units and first occupancy after 13 Mar 1991. Required features include an accessible entrance on an accessible route, accessible common areas, usable doors, accessible routes into and through units, accessible controls, reinforced bathroom walls for grab bars, and usable kitchens and bathrooms (read on justice.gov). Safe harbors and exact rules: to verify on hud.gov.
- Many states and cities add their own accessibility rules. Check the state building code agency (to verify).

### Construction safety (OSHA)

- Construction work is regulated by 29 CFR Part 1926. Read as a topic list on osha.gov: cranes and derricks, confined spaces, trenching and excavation, silica, PPE, demolition, electrical, residential construction. Fall protection, scaffolds and excavation subparts: to verify on ecfr.gov.
- OSHA hazards named on its construction page: falls, unguarded machinery, struck-by, electrocution, silica dust, asbestos.
- Design work: designers should flag construction hazards in notes where the project needs it. Employer duties stay with the contractor (to verify).
- In a State Plan state, use the state rules (see Legal hierarchy).

## Permits and approvals

Process varies by state and city. The AHJ is the local building department. Outline, to verify with the AHJ for each project:

| Phase | Approval | Issued by |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-design | Zoning check, land use approvals, variances | city or county planning department |
| Design | Building permit application with construction documents; plan review | building department (AHJ) |
| Design | Fire code review, sprinkler and fire alarm submittals | fire marshal or fire department |
| Design | Energy code compliance documents (for example COMcheck or REScheck, tools listed on energycodes.gov) | building department |
| Design | Design professional seal | state licensing board (architect, PE) |
| Construction | Separate trade permits (electrical, plumbing, mechanical) where required | building department |
| Construction | Inspections at stages (foundation, framing, rough-in, final) | building department |
| Closeout | Certificate of occupancy, sometimes temporary CO | building department |
| Special | Stormwater permits, lead RRP certification, environmental approvals | state agency, EPA |
| Special | Accessibility review (state or local), ADA compliance by the design team | AHJ, state agency |

Plan review basics: the applicant submits drawings, specifications and calculations. Reviewers return comments. The designer answers each comment and resubmits. Review times and fees vary (to verify). Some jurisdictions allow third-party plan review (to verify).

Project-level items: licensed professional must seal drawings where state law requires (to verify per state). Contractor licensing is state-specific (to verify).

## Units and conventions

- Units: US customary (imperial) in design documents: feet and inches, psf, psi, gpm, cfm, Btu/h, tons of refrigeration, degrees Fahrenheit. SI is used for some federal and metric projects.
- Conversion rules: 1 in = 25.4 mm exactly, 1 ft = 0.3048 m exactly. State the unit with every number. Show SI in brackets where the client needs it. Other factors: to verify (NIST, nist.gov, is the official source).
- Date format in documents: month/day/year. Use ISO 8601 (YYYY-MM-DD) for data and file names.
- Currency: USD.
- Document language: en-US.
- Classification: CSI MasterFormat for specifications (divisions), CSI UniFormat for elements and early estimates, OmniClass for some projects. Edition and division numbering: to verify.
- Drawings: ANSI/ARCH sheet sizes (Arch D 24x36 in is common), National CAD Standard in some offices (to verify).
- Contract forms (names only, not law): AIA Contract Documents, ConsensusDocs, EJCDC (engineers' documents), also DBIA. Check current versions with the publisher.
